# Day 01 — The Footprints That Vanished

> 《消失的第三十一页》第一幕：雾都谜案。**原创英语故事**，适合GPT独立朗读。以下正文完全不插中文，分三场景。听完再打开逐句精讲。

## Scene I — The Door Between Hours

The library appeared only after midnight. Lin Cheng knew this because she had searched the same narrow street every afternoon for eleven days and found nothing except a locked tailor's shop. Tonight, however, the shop window showed rows of books instead of coats, and a brass sign above the door read: THE LIBRARY OF UNFINISHED HISTORIES. Lin held the silver key her missing brother had left behind. She had promised herself not to use it until she could distinguish courage from desperation. But promises were easier to keep when nobody you loved had disappeared.

Inside, thousands of clocks ticked at different speeds. A woman with white gloves stood behind a desk, carefully repairing a torn photograph. “Your brother investigated a disappearance here,” she said, before Lin could introduce herself. “His initial approach was sensible, but he drew a conclusion too early.” The woman placed a folder in Lin's hands. Its label identified the case as London, 1894: The Footprints That Vanished. “The witness is probably telling the truth,” the woman added. “That does not mean she saw what she claims to have seen.” Before Lin could ask another question, the floor shifted beneath her feet.

## Scene II — A Witness at the Window

Cold rain struck Lin's face. She stood outside a brick house in a London courtyard, while a horse pulled an empty carriage past the iron gate. Inspector Gray, a tired man with remarkably clean boots, looked at the folder in her hands. “The library sent you,” he said, as if this explained everything. He led her towards a locked front door. Across the courtyard lay a strip of soft earth, soaked by hours of rain. There were no footprints in it. Not one.

“Elias Vale disappeared at eleven forty,” Gray explained. “A witness claims she observed him cross this ground and enter the house. Yet nobody opened the front door, and we can identify no other entrance.” Lin examined the earth. The smooth earth appeared to contradict the witness's claim, but near the wall Lin noticed a single pale thread caught on a nail. Apparently, someone had been there. Gray seemed pleased. “At last, evidence!”

“Not necessarily,” Lin replied. “A thread can indicate that clothing touched a nail. It cannot reveal when that happened, or who was wearing it.” The inspector frowned. “Are you suggesting we should ignore it?” “No. I am suggesting we should take the weather into account before we interpret it.” She asked to meet the witness.

Nora Keene, a seamstress who lived opposite the courtyard, waited beside an upstairs window. She was precise about one detail: at eleven forty, she had seen a dark figure move towards the front door. “I know what I saw,” Nora insisted. Lin did not question her honesty. Instead, she asked Nora to close the curtains, leaving a narrow opening, and describe the streetlamp's reflection in the glass. A passing cyclist appeared beside the house, although he was actually riding along the street behind them. Nora stepped back. “But that means—” “You saw a figure,” Lin said. “You assumed where that figure was.”

## Scene III — What the Rain Could Not Erase

They returned to the courtyard, where the rain had weakened to a whisper. Lin asked Gray to stand in the street while Nora watched from her window. When he walked past a certain lamp, his reflection crossed the glass in exactly the direction Nora remembered. In contrast to a person actually walking across the earth, however, a reflection would leave no footprints. Nora's account was reliable as a report of what she had seen; her interpretation of it was not.

Gray removed his hat. “Then our original theory was wrong. Our theory was based on an assumption: Vale entered the house. As a result, we looked for an impossible escape route.” “A mistaken assumption can have a significant consequence,” Lin said. “But it can also help us, once we identify it.” She pointed across the street. A narrow passage led behind a bakery. Vale could have walked along it without entering the courtyard at all.

Inside the passage, they discovered a wooden door with fresh scratches around its lock. Gray wanted to force it open, but Lin noticed a small envelope beneath the threshold. Written across it was a sentence in her brother's handwriting: THE MOST OBVIOUS EXPLANATION IS NOT ALWAYS THE CORRECT ONE. Her hands shook. She had followed the evidence to an address; somehow, her brother had been there first.

The envelope contained a photograph of Elias Vale standing next to Lin's brother. Both men looked directly at the camera, but a third person had been cut from the picture. On the back, in ink that had not yet dried, someone had written: FIRST PAGE RECOVERED. TWENTY-NINE REMAIN. Underneath was a second message: IF THE CLOCK STRIKES THIRTEEN, DO NOT TRUST THE TIME.

Lin suddenly understood why the librarian had warned her against quick conclusions. The missing person was not simply a victim waiting to be found; he might be following a plan. They could infer a possible route, not a motive. The clues could provide evidence for a planned disappearance, but they could not yet prove Vale's intention. Somewhere beyond the passage, a clock began to strike. Lin counted slowly, determined this time not to assume anything. Eleven. Twelve. Then came one more chime.
